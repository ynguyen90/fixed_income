Table 1. Why did Thailand fail to become a high-income country?

Thailand did not fail at industrialization. The core problem is that its industrialization did not translate into sufficient domestic technological capability, productivity growth, and innovation-led upgrading.

Research Argument Explanation for the Paper Suggested Source
Thailand industrialized successfully but growth slowed after reaching upper-middle-income status Thailand should not be viewed as a failed industrializer. It successfully built manufacturing and export capacity, but its long-term growth slowed after reaching upper-middle-income status. This suggests that industrialization alone is not sufficient to guarantee transition to high-income status. World Bank Thailand
Dependence on tourism, consumption, and fiscal stimulus Recent growth remains heavily supported by tourism recovery, private consumption, and government stimulus. These sources can support short-term demand, but they are not enough to generate the productivity acceleration needed for high-income transition. World Bank Thailand Economic Monitor
Weak domestic technological capability A core reason behind Thailand’s middle-income trap is the limited technological learning of domestic firms. Although Thailand attracted FDI and built an industrial base, domestic firms did not upgrade fast enough in technology, innovation, and higher-value production. Intarakumnerd
Global value chain participation without deep upgrading Thailand became deeply integrated into global value chains, especially in automobiles, electronics, and export manufacturing. However, participation in global production did not automatically lead to technology transfer, domestic value creation, or movement into design, R&D, branding, and advanced engineering. Korwatanasakul
Political economy and institutional constraints Institutions often protected incumbents and politically connected firms rather than creating strong incentives for productivity, competition, and domestic capability building. The middle-income trap is therefore not only an economic problem but also a political-economy problem. Doner & Schneider
Weak discipline in industrial policy Government support was not always tied to clear performance requirements, efficiency targets, or sunset clauses. Without strong conditionality, policy support can create rent-seeking and protect inefficient firms instead of forcing them to upgrade. ADB Institute
Loose financial supervision and short-term capital inflows before the 1997 crisis Weak financial supervision allowed cheap short-term foreign borrowing to flow into real estate and financial assets. This created an appearance of strong growth, but much of it was speculative and did not support long-term productivity-enhancing investment. Bank of Thailand
Need to move toward investment, infusion, and innovation For upper-middle-income economies, the next stage requires not only investment but also technology infusion and innovation. Thailand’s challenge is to move from FDI-led production toward domestic innovation capacity, stronger SMEs, and productivity-led growth. World Development Report 2024

Table 2. What is Vietnam doing to become a developed country and escape the middle-income trap?

Vietnam’s policy direction is shifting from “cheap labor + FDI + assembly + infrastructure” toward “productivity + technology + skilled labor + stronger domestic firms + better institutions.”

Policy Area What Vietnam Is Doing Why It Matters for Escaping the Middle-Income Trap Suggested Source
Selective, higher-quality FDI Vietnam is shifting from attracting FDI “at any cost” toward selecting projects with higher technology content, stronger domestic linkages, higher value added, R&D, worker training, and local supplier participation. Resolution 10-NQ/TW on foreign-invested economic development emphasizes quality, efficiency, technology transfer, and integration with domestic enterprises. This reduces the risk of an FDI trap, where the country attracts large foreign investment but remains concentrated in low-value assembly while core technology, design, branding, and profits stay abroad. Resolution 10-NQ/TW
Technology, innovation, and digital transformation Vietnam is promoting science, technology, innovation, and national digital transformation as strategic growth drivers. Resolution 57-NQ/TW frames these areas as breakthroughs for modern productive forces, governance modernization, and long-term growth. This is the transition from growth based mainly on capital and labor toward growth based on productivity, knowledge, data, and innovation. Without this shift, industrialization may remain shallow. Resolution 57-NQ/TW
Strategic industries such as semiconductors Vietnam has issued the semiconductor industry development strategy to 2030, with a vision to 2050. The strategy targets capabilities in research, design, manufacturing, packaging, testing, and a large pool of high-skilled semiconductor workers. Strategic sectors can help Vietnam move into higher-value parts of global supply chains. However, success requires a real domestic ecosystem: engineers, universities, labs, supplier firms, R&D, long-term finance, and intellectual-property capability. Decision 1018/QD-TTg
High-quality human capital Vietnam is prioritizing training in semiconductors, AI, data, automation, engineering, digital skills, and modern management. This goes beyond basic education and aims to prepare workers for higher-value industries and services. As wages rise and the population ages, Vietnam cannot depend only on abundant low-cost labor. Future growth must increasingly come from labor productivity, technical capability, and managerial upgrading. OECD Economic Survey: Viet Nam 2025
Domestic private-sector development Vietnam is trying to strengthen the domestic private sector through reforms that improve the business environment, reduce administrative barriers, support firm formalization, and encourage private firms to scale up and innovate. If Vietnamese firms do not become stronger, Vietnam may have high exports but low domestic value added. A developed economy needs competitive local firms that can own technology, brands, services, and supplier networks. World Bank: Viet Nam 2045
Supporting industries and mechanical engineering Vietnam is emphasizing supporting industries such as components, precision mechanics, industrial materials, logistics, packaging, industrial software, technical services, and equipment-related suppliers. This helps raise the domestic content of exports. It also allows Vietnamese firms to move from simple assembly toward production capability, supplier upgrading, and technology absorption. OECD: Trade, investment, and productivity
Better FDI spillovers and supplier linkages Vietnam is increasingly focusing on connecting FDI firms with domestic suppliers, improving supplier quality, raising production standards, and helping local firms meet multinational corporations’ requirements. FDI does not automatically transfer technology. Spillovers depend on domestic absorptive capacity, skilled labor, supplier relationships, quality standards, and incentives for multinationals to localize more complex activities. OECD
Public investment and infrastructure efficiency Vietnam is trying to accelerate public investment disbursement, decentralize project approval, simplify procedures, and improve implementation of infrastructure projects. The revised Public Investment Law aims to reduce bottlenecks in planning, approval, and project execution. Infrastructure supports productivity by reducing logistics costs, improving regional connectivity, and enabling industrial clusters. However, public investment must raise economy-wide productivity, not only boost short-term construction activity. Government Portal
Institutional reform and governance quality Vietnam is focusing on regulatory predictability, transparency, administrative simplification, capital-market development, public-investment quality, SOE reform, and stronger confidence for private investors. Institutions determine whether capital flows to productive firms or gets locked into low-productivity, speculative, or politically connected sectors. This is a key lesson from Thailand’s experience before and after the 1997 crisis. World Bank: Viet Nam 2045
Modern services, digital economy, and green transition Vietnam is broadening the growth model beyond manufacturing by developing logistics, finance, professional services, software, data-related services, clean energy, and adaptation to global green standards. Modern services and green transformation can become new sources of productivity growth. They also help Vietnam avoid being locked into low-margin manufacturing and carbon-intensive production. World Development Report 2024

Table 3. Is it possible?

The answer is yes, but it is not automatic. Vietnam’s high-income ambition is possible only if fast growth is converted into productivity, domestic technological capability, and stronger institutions.
